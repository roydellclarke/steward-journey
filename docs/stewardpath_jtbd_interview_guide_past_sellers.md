# StewardPath — Phase 0 JTBD Interview Guide (Past-Sellers)

> Companion to the current-owner guide. **Purpose split:** use THIS guide to discover and validate *the job* — what actually mattered, in their own words. Past-sellers have no live need, so they can't test willingness to pay; pair this with the current-owner guide for demand and price.

## How to use this (read first)
- **Talk 20%, listen 80%.** You're collecting a story, in sequence.
- **Reconstruct the real timeline, not the tidy one.** Hindsight rewrites memory. Anchor to concrete events and dates — "When exactly? What time of year? What else was going on?" — to rebuild what actually happened rather than the rationalized version.
- **Ask what happened, not what they wish.** Let regret and "I wish I'd…" surface on their own. If you fish for regret, you'll manufacture it.
- **Interview both ends.** Talk to people who sold *well* and prepared, not just the horror stories — what good preparation looked like is a description of your product's target outcome.
- **Probe the messy middle.** When someone says "it all worked out," gently ask what almost didn't.
- **Capture verbatim.** Their words about what mattered are your marketing language.
- **Respect limits.** Some have NDAs on terms or find it raw — you don't need exact numbers, just the shape and the feelings.

## Opening
- "Tell me about the business you sold — what it was, how long you ran it."
- "Give me the high-level version of how the sale came about — then I'll ask you to slow down on the parts that matter."

## 1. The first thought
- "Cast your mind back — when did selling *first* cross your mind? Not when you decided, just when the thought first appeared. What was going on then?"
- "How long was the gap between that first thought and actually doing something about it?"

## 2. The trigger — the push
- "What changed that moved it from a someday thought to something you acted on? Was there a specific event?"
- "Looking back, were you reacting to pressure — health, timing, a buyer showing up, fatigue — or was it on your own terms?"

## 3. What you actually did to prepare
- "Once you decided, what did you do first? Walk me through it step by step."
- "Who did you talk to — advisors, family, a broker — and in what order?"
- "What had to be in order before buyers got involved? What was a last-minute scramble?"

## 4. Choosing the buyer & the deal — the pull
- "How did you end up with the buyer you chose? Were there other options on the table?"
- "What made that one the one — and what almost killed it?"
- "How closely did the final terms match what you'd hoped for at the start?"

## 5. How it actually landed (the heart of it)
- "After it closed, what happened to your employees? Did it go the way you expected?"
- "Your customers — and the company name and reputation?"
- "How did your family experience the whole thing?"
- "How did the buyer treat what you'd built once they owned it?"
- "Was there anything you tried to protect in the deal? Did it hold?"

## 6. What mattered vs. what you expected (both directions — stays neutral)
- "Looking back, what turned out to matter most that you maybe underestimated going in?"
- "And the reverse — what did you worry about beforehand that turned out not to matter at all?"

## 7. The emotional arc
- "How did you feel in the months leading up to it? And right after it closed?"
- "Was there anything about letting go that surprised you?"

## 8. The value probe (open, not leading)
- "If a friend who owns a business told you they were starting to think about selling, what's the first thing you'd tell them?"
- "Was there any kind of help or preparation you wish had existed back then?"
- "Did you pay for help through the process — broker, advisor, planner? What, and was it worth it?"

## 9. Closing — and the bridge to current owners
- "What should I have asked you that I didn't?"
- "Do you know any owners who are earlier in this — still just thinking about it — who I should talk to?"

## Avoid (the hindsight traps specifically)
- Making "what do you wish you'd done?" your main question — let regret emerge on its own.
- Accepting the clean "it all worked out" arc without probing what nearly went wrong.
- Treating one dramatic exit as the pattern.
- Interviewing only the disasters — include the prepared, happy sellers.
- Talking about your solution.
